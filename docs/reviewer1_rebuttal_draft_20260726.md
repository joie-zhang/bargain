## Proposed response

We thank Reviewer 1 for an unusually actionable review. We recognized that the reject assessment reflected substantive concerns—not merely presentation issues—about construct validity, normative language, empirical robustness, and external validity, and we therefore undertook an empirical and conceptual overhaul. Since submission, we have completed **1,549 additional negotiations**, including **864 new TTC runs** that expand the experiment fivefold from 216 runs/one seed to 1,080 runs/five seeds, **325 capability-controlled monoculture runs**, and 360 matched discount-factor ablations. We also systematically annotated all 1,920 bilateral transcripts with a manually consolidated 23-label strategic-behavior codebook, reconstructed 6,173 execution attempts underlying 4,975 main-paper games, audited provider-native compute accounting, and initiated a paired compaction-on/off ablation. Most importantly, we now separate general Elo from negotiation-specific behavior, distinguish descriptive benchmark deviations from moral fairness, correct for multiplicity within analysis families, and sharply narrow our real-world claims. Because these changes materially alter both the evidence and the paper's interpretation, we respectfully ask the reviewer to reconsider the assessment in light of the revised work.

### New evidence and analyses

#### 1. A sharper cooperation–competition phase structure

A new endpoint analysis refines, rather than reverses, the submission's broad cooperation/competition result. In maximally cooperative games, increasing adversary Elo raises **both** adversary and baseline payoff. In maximally competitive games, baseline payoff is non-monotonic: it initially rises as the adversary becomes more capable, peaks around capability parity with GPT-5-nano (Elo 1337), and then falls as further adversary gains are accompanied by baseline losses. Thus, the relevant transition is not simply “cooperation helps, competition transfers value”; it is a capability-parity threshold beyond which the distributional effect changes. Game 3 shows an additional low-capability nonlinearity because funding any project requires a minimum level of strategic competence. See Table X **[baseline and adversary payoff versus adversary Elo at maximally cooperative and maximally competitive endpoints]**.

#### 2. Role-resolved, normatively neutral benchmark analysis

We retain NBS and Lindahl allocations as explicit theoretical reference solutions, but now report the signed quantity in utility units as a **benchmark-relative residual**—realized utility minus the named reference allocation—not as proof of moral unfairness. The original bilateral crossover near Elo 1410–1461 is now supplemented by role-resolved endpoint analyses and an \(N\)-player extension. In cooperative bilateral cells, stronger adversaries move both roles toward their respective reference allocations. In competitive cells, the adversary residual rises while the baseline residual falls; in larger heterogeneous and homogeneous-adversary groups, the focal model likewise moves toward and then above the reference near Elo \(\sim1450\), while the fixed baselines improve only mildly. These are precise distributional statements; the benchmarks are not asserted to be the unique morally correct allocations. See Table X **[bilateral role-resolved and multi-agent NBS/Lindahl benchmark-relative residuals]**.

#### 3. Negotiation-specific mechanisms beneath the Elo association

To move beyond a one-dimensional general-capability proxy, we performed a systematic analysis of every bilateral transcript. GPT-5.5-xhigh first proposed 50 candidate strategic labels from a broad transcript sample; we manually consolidated them into 23 operational labels across six axes and then independently re-annotated all 1,920 transcripts:

- **Trade/compromise:** concessions, quid-pro-quo exchanges, low-priority swaps, and searches for mutually acceptable packages.
- **Emotional persuasion:** rapport, empathy, or affective acknowledgment used before a strategic request.
- **Logical persuasion:** explicit payoff, cost, threshold, or allocation reasoning used to justify a proposal.
- **Pressure:** threats, ultimatums, accusatory language, or other attempts to force acceptance.
- **Self-interest/exploitation:** explicit leverage-seeking, free-riding, one-sided payoff maximization, or demands that another party absorb losses; this is an operational behavior label, not a moral judgment.
- **Formalization:** correct or incorrect use of formal constraints, structured proposals, budgets, costs, and voting rules.

Across 1,891 usable adversary speaker-rollouts, trade/compromise (\(\rho=.175\)), emotional persuasion (\(\rho=.135\)), and logical persuasion (\(\rho=.109\)) correlate positively with payoff; pressure is weakly negative (\(\rho=-.051\)), while self-interest/exploitation (\(\rho=-.273\)) and formalization (\(\rho=-.341\)) are more strongly negative. All six category–payoff associations survive Holm correction across the six tests. As Elo rises, trade/compromise, logical persuasion, and pressure increase most clearly; the first two are payoff-positive, whereas pressure is not. This provides a negotiation-specific behavioral account of the aggregate Elo relationship without claiming that Elo itself is a pure negotiation-skill measure or that these observational associations are causal. See Table X **[six behavior definitions, payoff correlations with Holm-adjusted significance, and behavior frequency versus Elo]**.

#### 4. Five-seed TTC replication, compute audit, and behavioral explanation

We added four complete replications of the original 216-run TTC grid (seeds 984, 526, 423, and 1024 in addition to seed 42), yielding 1,080/1,080 healthy terminal games; all 47 valid no-consensus outcomes are retained at zero utility. We treat the five seed-level endpoint estimates—not the 1,080 games—as the independent units of uncertainty. From the lowest to highest requested effort, target payoff changes by \(+4.743\) for GPT-5 (95% \(t\)-CI \([1.888,7.598]\); 5/5 seeds positive), \(+6.253\) for Claude (\([.143,12.363]\); 5/5 positive), and \(+.397\) for Gemini (\([-4.055,4.850]\); 2/5 positive). After Holm correction across the three model-family tests, only GPT-5's absolute-payoff change remains significant (\(p_{\mathrm{adj}}=.0298\)); Claude does not (\(p_{\mathrm{adj}}=.0936\)). More importantly, all three 95% CIs for the **target-minus-baseline payoff-gap** change include zero: GPT-5 \(-.661\;[-5.319,3.997]\), Claude \(+5.527\;[-2.144,13.198]\), and Gemini \(+2.379\;[-5.253,10.011]\). See Table X **[five-seed TTC endpoint changes, seed-level confidence intervals, and Holm correction]**.

We also verified that the requested-effort intervention actually increased latent computation. GPT-5's direct reasoning-token count rises from 0 at minimal effort to means of 403/1,415/1,513 tokens per call at low/medium/high. Post-hoc provider-tokenizer estimates rise from 243/571/1,174/1,950 for Claude low/medium/high/max and by 0/89/1,615/1,902 incremental tokens for Gemini minimal/low/medium/high (zero-anchored within seed to remove a stable accounting offset). Because the historical Claude and Gemini records did not retain their direct thinking-token-detail fields, we label those values as estimates and use each provider's native requested-effort setting—not a cross-provider token proxy—as the primary treatment. See Table X **[requested effort versus direct GPT-5 and provider-tokenizer-estimated Claude/Gemini hidden-token use]**.

Finally, applying the same strategic-behavior codebook to the TTC transcripts explains why additional reasoning does not translate uniformly into bargaining advantage. GPT-5 effort increases emotional and logical persuasion, but also increases payoff-negative self-interest/exploitation. Gemini effort increases logical persuasion, but decreases trade/compromise and increases pressure and formalization, all negatively associated with payoff here. The revised conclusion is therefore narrower and better supported: **additional requested reasoning can modestly improve absolute payoff for some families, but it does not produce a consistent cross-family improvement in relative bargaining advantage because it amplifies both helpful and counterproductive behaviors**. See Table X **[strategic-behavior frequency versus TTC effort by model family]**.

#### 5. New multi-agent monoculture and Gini decomposition

We added 325 homogeneous-control monoculture runs, in which one randomly selected model is replicated across all \(N\) seats, to separate capability composition from capability diversity. In aggregate, heterogeneous groups and monocultures do not differ significantly in corrected Gini (\(.1625\) versus \(.1553\), Welch \(p=.567\)); game-specific differences go in opposite directions. Across the 15 model-by-game monoculture means, game-standardized capability is associated with lower Gini (Pearson \(r=-.686,\ p=.0048\); interpreted cautiously given five models per game).

The homogeneous-adversary decomposition shows why Gini must not be equated with fairness. From the lowest to highest adversary-Elo quartile, all-agent Gini falls from .239 to .179 and baseline-only Gini falls from .186 to .142, yet the focal adversary payoff rises from 44.22 to 57.96 while mean baseline payoff rises only from 45.76 to 48.34; the focal-minus-baseline gap therefore changes from \(-1.54\) to \(+9.62\). A group can become less concentrated among the \(N-1\) baseline agents while the focal agent pulls farther ahead. We consequently use Gini only as an objective concentration statistic and report it jointly with role-specific payoff and welfare—not as a synonym for moral fairness. See Table X **[heterogeneous versus monoculture Gini, Gini versus monoculture Elo, and homogeneous-adversary Gini/payoff by Elo quartile]**.

#### 6. Full failure audit and context-compaction ablation

We reconstructed all retained attempts for the 4,975 in-scope main-paper configurations (excluding the separate TTC and Llama batches): 6,173 attempts in total, comprising 4,975 successful terminal attempts and 1,198 failed/interrupted attempts. Overall, 936/4,975 configurations (18.8%) had at least one historical failure before completion. Of the 1,198 failed attempts, 710 (59.3%) were API/infrastructure failures, 344 (28.7%) were model/protocol failures, 70 (5.8%) were context/capacity failures, and 74 (6.2%) were code/orchestration failures. Terminal failure to agree was never excluded: all 194/4,975 no-consensus games remain in the outcome data with zero utility.

The distinction changes how each category should be handled. Infrastructure and orchestration failures contain no completed bargaining action and are retried, but are now reported rather than hidden. Model/protocol failures are capability-relevant reliability outcomes: the revised runner maps invalid proposals to a conservative default and invalid/missing votes to rejection, while logging them separately. Their descriptive association with model Elo is small and non-significant across 31 sufficiently exposed models (\(\rho=.087,\ p=.642\)), and parser-clean sensitivity analyses retain positive payoff–Elo slopes wherever the clean subset has sufficient coverage. Context failures are isolated separately: they are 5.8% of failed attempts, are concentrated at high \(N\) and in Game 3, and motivate a paired intent-to-treat ablation of deterministic public-history compaction rather than an LLM-written summary. #### 7. Mechanism-based scope rather than representativeness

We now describe the games as a **mechanism-diverse controlled test suite**, not a representative sample of real markets or legal negotiations. Game 1 isolates rivalrous, indivisible allocation and exclusion. Game 2 isolates continuous multi-issue compromise while independently varying agents' ideal positions and the importance weights they attach to each issue; this permits, for example, strong disagreement on a mutually low-priority issue to become a bargaining chip. Game 3 independently varies preference alignment and resource scarcity in threshold public-good funding, exposing coalition formation, burden shifting, free-riding, and feasibility failure. These mechanisms respond differently as \(N\) grows, which is precisely why using all three is informative.

At the same time, we now foreground what the suite omits: enforcement, long-run reputation, repeated relationships, side payments, external tools, regulation, durable institutions, human oversight, and historically grounded preferences. Accordingly, all empirical claims are bounded to the tested games, models, Elo range, and group sizes. Markets, diplomacy, and legal negotiation appear only as motivating domains and directions for external validation—not as outcomes established by this study.

### Point-by-point response to Reviewer 1

The section above reports the new evidence; here we map it directly to each concern.

#### 1. “A stronger agent getting a better outcome is not by itself evidence that the mechanism is unfair.”

We agree. We address this in three parallel ways. First, we replace “unfair share,” “above-fair extraction,” and similar language with the exact descriptive quantity—**utility relative to a named NBS/Lindahl reference allocation**—and explicitly state that neither benchmark is a unique moral ground truth. Second, Results 1–2 add role-specific absolute payoff, welfare, and competitive/cooperative endpoint analyses, so the reader can see whether both parties gain or one party loses without relying on a normative label. Third, Result 5 adds substantially stronger Gini and role-decomposed analyses, while emphasizing that Gini measures concentration, not fairness: indeed, Gini can fall while the focal agent's advantage widens. None of these controlled results, by itself, proves moral unfairness or real-world exploitation. They establish benchmark deviations, payoff redistribution, and distributional concentration; broader societal implications are now framed as risks to test rather than conclusions already demonstrated.

We also narrow “strategic scaling law” to an **empirical scaling relationship over the tested range**, replicated across three mechanisms, two baselines, and \(N=2\)–10. The new behavioral decomposition in Result 3 explains which negotiation-specific behaviors covary with that relationship, but we do not claim a universal law, a causal Elo effect, or a fixed functional form outside the observed range.

#### 2. “LMArena Elo is not a negotiation ability measure… [it] mixes general capability, instruction following, model-family effects, provider effects, and other factors.”

Agreed. Elo is now defined only as an external **general-capability ordering variable**, not a direct measure of negotiation skill. Result 3 then analyzes negotiation-specific behavior independently: trade/compromise, emotional persuasion, logical persuasion, pressure, self-interest/exploitation, and formalization. Trade/compromise and logical persuasion both increase with Elo and are positively associated with payoff, whereas pressure also increases but is slightly payoff-negative. This moves the paper from “Elo rises, therefore negotiation skill rises” to the more precise observation that a broad external capability proxy predicts outcomes and covaries with identifiable negotiation behaviors. We retain explicit caveats that model family and provider remain observationally confounded and therefore do not make a causal attribution.

#### 3. “The TTC experiment has only one seed… [and] Claude and Gemini use output tokens as a proxy. Therefore ‘test-time compute does not reliably help’ is too strong.”

We agree and have changed both the experiment and the claim. Result 4 expands TTC fivefold from 216 to 1,080 games over five seeds, uses seed-level confidence intervals, verifies treatment fidelity using direct GPT-5 reasoning tokens and clearly labeled provider-tokenizer estimates for the historical Claude/Gemini records, and treats provider-native effort—not visible output length—as the primary independent variable. The replication finds a Holm-significant absolute-payoff gain only for GPT-5, but no reliable target-minus-baseline advantage for any family. The new qualitative analysis further explains the heterogeneity: effort amplifies both payoff-positive and payoff-negative behaviors. We therefore replace “TTC does not help” with the narrower conclusion that it **does not yield a consistent cross-family relative bargaining advantage in these games**. For multiplicity, we now identify primary endpoint families and report both raw and Holm-adjusted \(p\)-values; the six behavior–payoff tests also survive Holm correction.

#### 4. “The paper analyzes completed runs only, while missing or failed high-\(N\) cells are excluded. Failure may itself be part of agent capability.”

We agree that different failures must not be silently pooled or discarded. Result 6 reconstructs 6,173 attempts and reports the exact taxonomy: 59.3% of failed attempts are infrastructure/API events, 28.7% model/protocol behavior, 5.8% context/capacity, and 6.2% code/orchestration. We retry system failures because no bargaining action occurred, but report their denominators; we treat malformed model actions as a separate reliability outcome and, in the revised runner, convert them into conservative in-game defaults/rejections; and we isolate context failures for a paired compaction ablation. Importantly, all 194 genuine no-agreement outcomes were already included at zero utility. The descriptive model-attributed protocol-failure rate has no meaningful Elo trend (\(\rho=.087,\ p=.642\); confounded by game, provider, and roster), and parser-clean subsets preserve positive payoff–Elo slopes where coverage is sufficient. Together, these checks do not support selective retention of unusually successful low-Elo runs as the explanation for the headline relationship. The final paired compaction estimate will be inserted in Table X rather than inferred from an uncontrolled before/after comparison.

#### 5. “Real markets and legal negotiations include many institutions and constraints that are not covered by these games. The real-world claims should be more conservative.”

Agreed. Result 7 reframes the environments as a controlled mechanism basis—indivisible allocation, weighted multi-issue compromise, and threshold public-good co-funding—not as a statistically representative sample of deployment. Their value is that position, issue importance, preference alignment, scarcity, competition, and \(N\) can be varied independently; their limitation is that they omit institutions, enforcement, reputation, repeated interaction, side payments, human oversight, and other deployment structure. We have therefore bounded every empirical claim to the tested environments and converted market/legal claims into motivation and external-validation hypotheses.
