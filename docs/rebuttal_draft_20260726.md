## Draft response

We thank the reviewers for their detailed and actionable feedback. We took the concerns as a request for both stronger evidence and more precisely bounded claims, and the resulting revision is materially different from the submitted paper. Relative to the submission's 5,387-run evidence base, we can now add 1,549 completed negotiations: 864 additional test-time-compute runs, expanding that experiment from 216 runs and one seed to 1,080 runs and five seeds; 360 matched discount-factor ablations across \(\gamma\in\{0.5,0.9,1.0\}\); and 325 random-monoculture controls. We also systematically annotated all 1,920 bilateral transcripts and the original 216 TTC transcripts with a 23-label strategic-behavior codebook, reconstructed 6,173 execution attempts underlying 4,975 in-scope main-paper games, and reanalyzed all 2,730 multi-agent runs (16,380 agent observations). These additions do more than increase sample size: they refine the TTC conclusion, identify negotiation-specific behaviors associated with capability and payoff, separate benchmark-relative distribution from normative fairness, and explain when and why capability–payoff slopes change with group size. We summarize each change below.

### 1. Fairness terminology and distributional claims [R1]

We agree with the reviewer that a stronger agent receiving more utility does not, by itself, establish moral unfairness. The Nash Bargaining Solution and Lindahl-style allocation are principled cooperative-game-theoretic reference points, but they are not uniquely authoritative definitions of fairness. We will therefore rename the “fair-share residual” to the more descriptive **benchmark-relative utility residual**, define it simply as realized utility minus the relevant NBS/Lindahl allocation, and remove language that equates a positive residual with coercion, deception, exploitation, or moral unfairness.

We have also expanded the distributional analysis in three ways. First, the benchmark-relative residual rises with Elo and crosses zero at approximately Elo 1410–1461 in the bilateral games and approximately 1450 in the multi-agent analysis. We will describe this only as movement from below to above a specified reference allocation. Second, we separately analyze each role's absolute payoff. In cooperative settings, increasing adversary capability raises both agents' payoffs; in maximally competitive settings, GPT-5-nano's payoff rises only until the adversary approaches capability parity (GPT-5-nano Elo 1337) and then declines. Thus, the strongest deployment-relevant result is not the normative label attached to a benchmark: it is that marginal capability increases beyond parity can redistribute payoff away from the weaker agent in competitive environments. Third, we add a value-neutral Gini analysis using 325 random-monoculture controls. Pooled heterogeneous groups are not significantly more unequal than pooled monocultures; instead, monoculture capability predicts inequality, with stronger monocultures reaching lower-Gini outcomes. At the same time, increasing only one focal agent's capability can lower group Gini by compressing the \(N-1\) weaker agents' payoffs while the focal agent's own payoff and gap over them increase. This demonstrates why lower Gini should not automatically be interpreted as a morally fairer outcome.

Accordingly, the revised paper distinguishes (i) benchmark-relative allocation, (ii) payoff inequality, (iii) total welfare, and (iv) changes in the weaker party's absolute payoff. We will use “fairness” only when explicitly referring to a named theoretical benchmark and will state that the normative interpretation remains benchmark-dependent.

### 2. What “capability” and LMArena Elo measure [R1, R2]

We agree that the original presentation did not sufficiently distinguish the construct of capability from its operational proxy. The revision will define three separate concepts:

- **General model capability:** broad competence across tasks.
- **Negotiation-specific capability:** behaviors useful for bargaining, including identifying trades, reasoning about utilities, tracking concessions, persuading other agents, and executing the protocol reliably.
- **Operational capability measure:** LMArena Elo, used as an external model-ordering variable rather than treated as a ground-truth latent measure of strategic skill.

We will therefore state the headline association precisely: within these environments, higher LMArena Elo predicts higher bargaining payoff. We do not claim that Elo cleanly isolates negotiation skill or causally identifies the source of the payoff relationship.

To investigate what connects Elo to negotiation outcomes, we conducted a systematic transcript analysis rather than relying on selected examples. GPT-5.5-xhigh judges first generated 50 candidate strategic labels; we manually consolidated them into a non-overlapping 23-label codebook spanning six categories—pressure, emotional persuasion, logical persuasion, trade/compromise, self-interest/exploitation, and formalization—and independently re-annotated all 1,920 bilateral transcripts. Across 1,912 usable adversary speaker-rollouts, trade/compromise is the strongest positive correlate of payoff (\(\rho=0.170\)), followed by emotional persuasion (\(\rho=0.140\)) and logical persuasion (\(\rho=0.117\)). Overt self-interest/exploitation is negatively associated with payoff (\(\rho=-0.255\)); pressure is only weakly negative (\(\rho=-0.054\)). Higher-Elo models use pressure, trade/compromise, and logical persuasion more frequently, but only the latter two are meaningfully payoff-positive. The resulting mechanism is more specific than “better models do better”: stronger agents appear particularly better at identifying mutually beneficial trades and articulating logically persuasive agreements, while overtly adversarial self-advocacy is not what predicts success.

We also use the execution audit to test a simpler alternative explanation: that Elo merely measures structured-output compliance. Across 31 models with sufficient exposure, the association between Elo and model-attributed terminal protocol-failure rate is small and non-significant (Spearman \(\rho=0.087,\ p=0.642\)). The positive payoff relationship also replicates with Llama 3.3 70B rather than GPT-5-nano as the fixed baseline. These analyses do not fully disentangle provider and family effects, and we will say so explicitly, but they make clear that the observed payoff gradient is not reducible to a simple format-compliance gradient.

### 3. Test-time compute: seeds and provider-specific measurement [R1]

We agree that one seed was insufficient for the original TTC conclusion. We added four complete 216-run replications, using seeds 984, 526, 423, and 1024 in addition to the original seed 42. The resulting experiment contains 1,080/1,080 healthy completed games across five seeds, three model families, four provider-native effort settings, nine matched game cells, and both agent orders. Forty-seven terminal no-consensus outcomes are retained as valid outcomes rather than removed.

We use the five independent seed-level endpoint estimates as the unit of uncertainty, rather than treating all 1,080 runs as independent. From the lowest to highest requested effort, GPT-5's target payoff increases by \(+4.743\) points (95% Student-\(t\) CI across seeds \([1.888,7.598]\); positive in 5/5 seeds), and Claude Sonnet 4.6 increases by \(+6.253\) (\([0.143,12.363]\); positive in 5/5 seeds). Gemini 3 Flash changes by only \(+0.397\) (\([-4.055,4.850]\); positive in 2/5 seeds). Crucially, the 95% intervals for the **target-minus-baseline payoff gap** cross zero for all three families. The new evidence therefore changes and sharpens our conclusion: greater requested effort can increase the target agent's own payoff for GPT-5 and Claude in these settings, but the effect does not generalize to Gemini and does not establish a reliable improvement in relative bargaining advantage for any family.

We also agree that token accounting is not comparable across providers. The primary treatment is now each provider's native requested-effort setting. GPT-5's explicitly reported reasoning tokens are shown as a family-specific diagnostic; Claude and Gemini visible output tokens are labeled as such and are not presented as estimates of hidden reasoning. We do not infer unobserved reasoning tokens by subtracting visible output from an ambiguous total.

Finally, applying the same 23-label analysis to the original 216 TTC transcripts helps explain the family heterogeneity. Greater GPT-5 effort increases emotional and logical persuasion but also self-interested behavior. Greater Gemini effort increases logical persuasion, formalization, and pressure while reducing trade/compromise. Thus, additional reasoning can amplify both helpful and payoff-negative strategic behaviors. We will replace the original broad statement that “test-time compute does not help” with the narrower evidence-supported conclusion above.

### 4. Completed-run selection, model failures, and context handling [R1]

We agree that failures may contain capability information and should not be hidden by conditioning silently on completed runs. We therefore reconstructed the complete retained attempt history for the 4,975 in-scope main-paper games, excluding the separate TTC and Llama batches. The audit contains 6,173 execution attempts: 4,975 successful terminal attempts and 1,198 failed or interrupted attempts. In total, 936/4,975 configurations (18.8%) experienced at least one historical failure before completion.

The failure taxonomy is important. Of the 1,198 failed attempts, 710 (59.3%) are API/infrastructure failures, 344 (28.7%) are model/protocol failures such as invalid proposals, votes, or truncated outputs, 70 (5.8%) are context/capacity failures, and 74 (6.2%) are code/orchestration failures. We now report both attempt-level and configuration-level denominators and break them down by model, Elo, provider, game, \(N\), competition condition, and round. Terminal no-consensus is not classified as a failure or excluded: all 194/4,975 no-consensus games remain in the outcome dataset with zero analysis utility, so the existing payoff plots do not require correction.

For the model-caused component most relevant to the reviewer's selection concern, model-attributed protocol-failure rate has no clear relationship with Elo (\(\rho=0.087,\ p=0.642\)). This does not prove that execution reliability and bargaining capability are causally unrelated, but it makes a simple explanation in which the payoff slope is produced by selectively retaining unusually successful low-Elo runs less plausible. We will report reliability as a distinct outcome rather than assigning provider outages an artificial zero bargaining utility.

We have also documented the context intervention precisely. The mitigation is deterministic public-history compaction—not an LLM-generated free-form summary—and retains compact discussion records, formal proposals, and votes. Among 3,055 in-scope multi-agent configurations, 42 had a retained historical context failure and 122 used proactive compaction in the final completed rollout. The historical context-failure rate decreased from 41/2,547 attempts before the intervention to 12/1,506 afterward, but because model rosters and dates differ, we will report this only descriptively and will not claim behavioral equivalence from that before/after comparison.

### 5. Why these games, and limits on real-world generalization [R1, R2]

We agree that the three games are not a statistically representative sample of real-world negotiation. The revised paper will describe them as a **mechanism-diverse controlled test suite** selected to span three distinct strategic structures:

- **Item allocation:** rivalrous and indivisible resources, where allocating an item to one agent excludes the others.
- **Treaty negotiation:** continuous, divisible compromise over multiple issues, implemented through a supermajority agreement.
- **Participatory budgeting:** threshold public goods with shared benefits, endogenous cost sharing, coalition formation, and opportunities for free-riding.

This selection lets us vary capability, competition, and group size while holding the game mechanism controlled, and the mechanisms produce empirically distinct behavior: inequality rises most strongly with \(N\) in item allocation; treaty bargaining compresses individual payoff differences through continuous compromise; and participatory budgeting is dominated by feasibility, spillovers, and contribution burdens.

We will remove or qualify claims that these games directly represent deployment. The revised scope is “within these controlled environments” and “a deployment risk suggested by these results,” not a universal claim about markets, diplomacy, or institutions. We will also state the omitted features explicitly: enforceable contracts, outside options, side payments, repeated interaction, reputation, durable institutions, regulation, human oversight, and long-term coalitions. Controlled games remain useful because they isolate mechanisms that are difficult to vary independently in live deployments, but validation in richer human and institutional settings is future work.

### 7. Sensitivity to the utility discount factor [R3]

We agree that post-hoc removal of the discount does not test whether agents would negotiate differently under another discount regime. We therefore ran a new matched Game 1 ablation with 360/360 completed negotiations: ten target models spanning the Elo range, three competition levels, both orders, two matched seeds, and \(\gamma\in\{0.5,0.9,1.0\}\). Only the discount factor and its truthful prompt description change.

The capability–allocation relationship remains positive under every regime. The target model's pre-discount allocation-utility slopes are \(+3.39\), \(+4.18\), and \(+4.02\) utility per 100 Elo for \(\gamma=0.5,0.9,1.0\), respectively (Pearson \(r=0.68,0.85,0.87\)). Relative to \(\gamma=0.9\), the strong \(\gamma=0.5\) treatment mechanically lowers realized social welfare by 14.11 points, but pre-discount allocation welfare changes by only \(+0.192\pm3.436\) (95% CI half-width), final agreement round by \(+0.025\pm0.282\), and agreement probability by \(0.000\pm0.023\). Likewise, \(\gamma=1.0\) changes pre-discount allocation welfare by only \(-0.042\pm2.877\), final round by \(+0.100\pm0.275\), and agreement probability by \(0.000\pm0.023\), relative to \(\gamma=0.9\).

Thus, discounting changes realized utility through its mathematical multiplier, as expected, but the negotiated allocations, timing, agreement probability, and positive capability relationship are stable across the tested regimes. We will add the full matched analysis and clarify that \(\gamma=0.9\) was chosen to make delay costly, not because the headline scaling result depends on that particular value.

### 8. Why the capability–payoff slope changes with \(N\) [R3]

We thank the reviewer for identifying the apparent flattening. We reanalyzed all 2,730 multi-agent runs and 16,380 agent observations, separating two designs that the original figure visually pooled: (i) one varied focal adversary negotiating with \(N-1\) fixed GPT-5-nano agents, and (ii) heterogeneous random rosters.

The decline is not universal. In the controlled focal-adversary design, the raw payoff slopes from \(N=2\) to \(N=10\) change from \(6.36\) to \(10.84\) in item allocation, \(4.30\) to \(4.44\) in treaty negotiation, and \(1.59\) to \(3.08\) in participatory budgeting. Thus, adding more fixed weak co-players does not systematically dilute the focal agent's capability advantage. In heterogeneous rosters, the \(N=10\) model-mean slope is numerically lower in all three games, but only participatory budgeting has a clearly nonzero endpoint decline (\(-3.70\), bootstrap 95% CI \([-6.90,-0.66]\)).

A within-run analysis gives the cleanest comparison because it relates each agent's Elo advantage over its actual co-players to its payoff advantage within the same realized game. For every two additional agents, the Elo-by-\(N\) interaction is \(+0.30\) in item allocation (95% CI \([-0.37,0.97], p=.383\)), \(-0.44\) in treaty negotiation (\([-0.84,-0.04], p=.030\)), and \(-1.14\) in participatory budgeting (\([-1.72,-0.55], p=1.5\times10^{-4}\)). The mechanism is therefore game-specific:

- **Item allocation:** the apparent raw flattening is payoff-scale compression. Equal-share optimal welfare \(SW^\star/N\) falls 43% as more agents divide rivalrous goods. After normalizing by \(NU_i/SW^\star\), the model-mean Elo slope rises from 0.092 at \(N=2\) to 0.213 at \(N=10\).
- **Treaty negotiation:** the decline is moderate and ecology-dependent. Within-run payoff dispersion falls 27% (8.45 to 6.16), consistent with continuous compromise and supermajority aggregation reducing any one heterogeneous agent's influence. The controlled focal-agent slope remains stable.
- **Participatory budgeting:** the decline is large and substantive. Higher-Elo agents continue to receive more project benefit, but as \(N\) increases they also bear more cost. The within-run benefit slope decreases from 8.16 to 4.45 per 100 Elo, while the contribution-cost slope triples from 1.05 to 3.19, reducing the net-payoff slope from 7.11 to 1.25. Capability increasingly generates group spillovers and threshold-closing contributions rather than purely private surplus.

The result is not explained by high-\(N\) disagreement or longer deliberation: heterogeneous consensus reaches 100% at \(N=10\) in all three games, and agreement rounds do not increase systematically. We will revise the paper to state the more precise finding: group size does not mechanically weaken capability scaling; it changes how capability is converted into private payoff, depending on the game mechanism and capability composition of the group.

### 9. Linear versus nonlinear scaling relationships

We agree that a linear trend alone should not be presented as proof of a universal functional scaling law. We compared linear and quadratic relationships using model-level bilateral means, AIC, and leave-one-model-out cross-validation. In item allocation, the linear model is preferred (AIC 158.07 versus 159.99; LOOCV RMSE 3.31 versus 3.42; quadratic-term \(p=.789\)). The same is true in participatory budgeting (AIC 199.99 versus 201.98; RMSE 7.63 versus 8.38; \(p=.930\)). Treaty negotiation shows modest possible curvature: the quadratic improves AIC from 168.93 to 165.99 and LOOCV RMSE from 4.19 to 4.15, with an uncorrected quadratic-term \(p=.0366\). Across the three game-level curvature tests, however, this corresponds to \(q\approx.110\), so we do not treat it as definitive evidence for a general nonlinear form.

We will report the alternative fits with uncertainty rather than showing only linear trend lines. More importantly, we will narrow the terminology: the paper establishes robust empirical capability–outcome relationships over the observed Elo range, not a universal power law in Elo. Elo has an arbitrary origin, so we will not fit a power law to raw Elo without theoretical justification. The current evidence supports approximate linearity over the tested range in item allocation and participatory budgeting, with modest possible curvature in treaty negotiation; it does not support a common accelerating or diminishing-return law across all games.

We will also distinguish the small set of confirmatory headline comparisons from exploratory mechanism analyses, emphasize effect sizes and confidence intervals, and apply false-discovery-rate correction within families of related tests.

### Closing

The reviews led us to strengthen both the evidence and the claims. The revised paper adds 1,549 completed negotiations not present in the submitted evidence base, expands the TTC study fivefold, introduces a matched three-regime discount-factor ablation and randomized monoculture controls, audits the complete retained attempt history, replaces selected qualitative examples with systematic transcript annotation, and supplies a mechanism-level explanation of the group-size result. Just as importantly, we revise claims where the new evidence requires it: TTC effects are family- and outcome-dependent, “fairness” is benchmark-relative rather than a moral conclusion, Elo is an operational proxy rather than a pure measure of strategic skill, and the observed relationships are not asserted to be universal power laws or direct models of real-world deployment. We hope the reviewers will reassess the paper in light of this substantially expanded evidence and the more precise conclusions it supports.
